The cheapest way to serve a big model right now is starting to look like a box of workstation GPUs. A card in the RTX Pro 6000 class has plenty of memory and compute for what it costs. What it doesn't have is NVLink, so the GPUs talk to each other over PCIe, and once a model needs more than one box, over Ethernet.

I spent the fall in Astera Labs' CTO office measuring what that actually costs. The setup was two 8-GPU PCIe boxes joined by 400G RoCE Ethernet through a single switch. We served MoE models (GLM-5.3-Flash, Qwen3.5-122B and a few others) with vLLM on 4, 8 and 16 GPUs, and profiled every decode step.

<Diagram name="rig" caption="The rig. Inside a box, GPUs talk over PCIe; any tensor-parallel group bigger than eight has to cross the Ethernet switch." />

This post is about one question that kept coming up: when a tensor-parallel all-reduce is slow, what is it actually spending its time on? I went in expecting the answer to be bandwidth, or GPUs waiting on each other. It was neither. Everything here is tensor parallelism; expert parallelism and its all-to-all traffic were out of scope.

## Why tensor parallelism needs an all-reduce

Tensor parallelism splits every weight matrix across N GPUs. Each GPU multiplies its own slice and ends up with a partial result, and those partial results have to be summed before the next layer can start. That sum is an all-reduce, and a standard transformer layer does two of them: one after attention, one after the MLP.

<Diagram name="tp-block" caption="One block split across four GPUs. Each produces a partial result; the all-reduce sums them so every GPU has the full output before the next block." />

Two things make it expensive. It sits on the critical path, since nothing downstream can run until it finishes. And it happens on every layer of every step. GLM-5.3-Flash makes 91 all-reduces per decode step and Qwen3.5-122B makes 96, about two per layer, and that count is fixed by the architecture, so a longer prompt doesn't change it.

On these boxes, every one of those calls goes through NCCL. vLLM ships its own custom all-reduce kernel, but it turns itself off for more than two GPUs that only have PCIe between them. So whatever NCCL decides to do is your latency.

## A simple model of the cost

For all but the very smallest messages, NCCL's default here is a ring ([part 2](/blog/nccl-defaults-off-nvlink) covers the Tree exception across boxes). The GPUs form a loop and the message is cut into N chunks. For N−1 steps, each GPU passes a chunk to its neighbour and adds in the one it receives, until every chunk is fully summed somewhere on the ring. For another N−1 steps, the finished chunks travel around until every GPU has all of them.

<Diagram name="ring-allreduce" caption="Ring all-reduce on four GPUs: three steps to build one fully summed chunk per GPU, three more to pass them around." />

Each of those 2(N−1) steps has a fixed cost (launching the work, signalling the neighbour, waiting for its flag) plus the time to move one chunk. Add them up and you get the textbook model:

$$
t \;\approx\; \underbrace{2(N-1)\,\alpha}_{\text{startup}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{2(N-1)}{N}\cdot\frac{n}{B}}_{\text{transfer}}
$$

α is the fixed cost per step, n is the message size and B is the link bandwidth. The first term doesn't care how big the message is; the second grows with it. Which one wins depends entirely on n.

<Diagram name="alpha-beta" caption="The same six ring steps at two message sizes. A small message pays almost nothing but startup." />

## Decode messages are tiny

Each all-reduce carries the activations for the tokens in the step, so its size is easy to work out:

$$
\text{message bytes} = \text{tokens in step} \times \text{hidden dim} \times \text{bytes per activation}
$$

In decode, each running request adds one token per step. With a hidden size of 4096 in bf16, that's 8 KiB per request in flight: a single request sends 8 KiB all-reduces, and 512 concurrent requests send 4 MiB. Prefill is a different world. An 8,192-token prefill chunk sends 64 MiB per all-reduce, so a single serving session sweeps the whole range.

<Diagram name="message-sizes" caption="All-reduce sizes for hidden size 4096 in bf16. Decode lives between kilobytes and a few megabytes; one prefill chunk is far past that." />

NCCL also splits each all-reduce across several channels, each running its own ring on a slice of the message, so every ring step moves a fraction of an already small chunk. Plug a few kilobytes into the model and the transfer term all but disappears. That was my hypothesis going in, and the rest of this post checks it against real decode steps.

## Splitting real all-reduces into three parts

For every all-reduce in a captured decode step, I split the measured time into three pieces. Startup is what the same call costs at 256 bytes, using whichever algorithm and protocol NCCL actually picked; that's the α term. Transfer is what an isolated benchmark adds on top of that at the real message size. Whatever is left over, the in-run time the benchmark can't explain, I called residual wait. If GPUs were stalling on late peers, it would show up there.

<Diagram name="chart-breakdown" caption="How all-reduce time splits at each load level, GLM-5.3-Flash with CUDA graphs. Left, one 8-GPU box; right, 16 GPUs across two boxes over RoCE." />

At low load it's almost all startup. With a handful of requests in flight, each all-reduce carries a few kilobytes, so there's nothing to transfer and the time is fixed cost, just like the model says. Below a few hundred kilobytes the latency curve is flat. Inside one box, transfer only overtakes startup once a few hundred requests are running.

Startup also scales with the number of ring steps. Every GPU you add to the group adds a roughly constant slice of latency to every call, however small the message.

The right half of the chart has a twist: across two boxes, startup takes over again at the highest loads. That isn't physics. At a certain size NCCL switches protocol, and the new one has a much higher fixed cost per call. Why that switch happens in the wrong place without NVLink is what [part 2](/blog/nccl-defaults-off-nvlink) is about.

## Waiting on each other wasn't the problem

The team's earlier profiles said most communication time was spin-wait: GPUs sitting idle until slower peers caught up. I fully expected this project to be about stragglers. It wasn't. Those profiles had been taken in eager mode, where the gaps between kernel launches get counted as communication. In CUDA-graph captures, which is how vLLM actually serves, residual wait inside one box is a thin sliver at every load.

Across Ethernet a real residual does show up at medium load, and it traced back to two specific things. The first all-reduce of every graph replay pays to wake up NCCL's network proxy thread, a fixed charge once per decode step that's small inside a box and much larger across Ethernet. And Ring/LL across the two boxes runs at one of two speeds, decided when the process launches and stuck for its lifetime. I never pinned down why, so if you benchmark across hosts, run more than one launch before believing a number.

Genuine rank-to-rank jitter was a few microseconds, and slack in the step absorbed it. The practical lesson is to profile in graph mode before going after stragglers. An eager-mode profile will happily show you a problem your server doesn't have.

## Ethernet costs latency, not bandwidth

<Diagram name="chart-latency" caption="Time per all-reduce against message size (log-log), NCCL defaults. Shaded: the sizes decode actually sends." />

Start at the right edge of this chart. At large messages the two curves meet, because the bottleneck is each GPU's own PCIe Gen5 x16 link, not the network. Ethernet doesn't lose on bandwidth at all. Now look at the shaded band where decode lives: that's where the gap is. Crossing Ethernet adds fixed cost to every call, and decode is almost all fixed cost.

So where does that extra cost come from? Not the wire. The fabric ran near line rate, and NIC hardware timestamps put the switch and cables at a small slice of each crossing. Most of it is on the hosts: NCCL's proxy thread, the NIC's side of PCIe, and turning GPU writes into RDMA operations.

<Diagram name="eth-crossing" caption="One crossing between boxes. The wire and the switch are a small slice; most of the fixed cost is the host-side path and the proxy thread that drives it." />

One NIC detail surprised me. By default NCCL sent all cross-host ring traffic through a single NIC per host and left the rest idle, because behind a PCIe switch every NIC looks equally close to every GPU. For all-reduce it barely matters, though. A ring across two hosts enters and leaves each host at exactly one GPU, so it can use at most two NICs per host. Extra rails help all-to-all and all-gather, which is MoE expert-parallel traffic, not tensor-parallel traffic.

<Diagram name="two-nics" caption="A 16-GPU ring crosses between the boxes in exactly two places, so at most two NICs per box ever see its traffic." />

## More GPUs, more latency

<Diagram name="chart-share" caption="Communication's share of a GLM-5.3-Flash decode step at TP4 and TP8 in one box, and TP16 across two boxes over Ethernet." />

Going from 4 to 8 to 16 GPUs, the bytes each GPU sends per step barely change: 2(N−1)/N of the message goes from 1.5× to 1.75× to about 1.9×. Everything else does change. Each GPU has less compute to do, because the weights are split more ways. Ring steps per call go from 6 to 14 to 30. And at 16 GPUs, every call also crosses Ethernet twice.

So communication's share of the step climbs with every doubling, then jumps when the group crosses the network; at medium load across Ethernet it rivals compute. None of that growth is bandwidth. It's the startup term, multiplied.

## What I'd do on a box without NVLink

Keep tensor parallelism inside one PCIe box and scale out with independent replicas. When the model fits in one box at TP8, two separate TP8 servers on our 16 GPUs beat one TP16 server over Ethernet at every concurrency we tried, because nothing has to cross the network on every step. For long-context MLA models bound by KV-cache capacity, [part 3](/blog/pcie-vs-ethernet-scale-up) covers splitting the model into two pipeline stages with TP8 in each box.

Profile in CUDA-graph mode, since eager mode inflates communication and invents spin-wait. When you benchmark with nccl-tests, force the same algorithm and protocol your server uses (`NCCL_ALGO`, `NCCL_PROTO`); compared like that, per-call time inside vLLM matched the isolated benchmark closely. Don't buy extra NIC rails hoping to speed up tensor-parallel all-reduce, because a two-host ring can't use them.

And check which protocol NCCL actually picked, with `NCCL_DEBUG=INFO NCCL_DEBUG_SUBSYS=TUNING`. Without NVLink it's often the wrong one, and fixing that turned out to be the single biggest win of the project. That's [part 2](/blog/nccl-defaults-off-nvlink).

## How I measured it

Decode steps came from Nsight Systems captures of vLLM in CUDA-graph mode, with kernels classified through CUPTI correlation IDs. Startup and transfer came from an isolated harness on vLLM's own PyNccl communicator: 50 bf16 all-reduces captured in one CUDA graph, median of 60 replays, slowest rank. The `torch.distributed` path adds a per-call cost that grows with graph length, so it's worth matching whichever path your server uses. Message sizes and counts came from eager runs with the NCCL profiler plugin (graph replays don't emit profiler events) and were checked against the formula above, with nccl-tests sweeps as a cross-check.
