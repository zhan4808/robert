<Section id="motivation" title="Motivation">
  <Note title="correction">
    Corrected November 2026. An earlier version of this page blamed the INT4 result on the 16 MB weights living in H100's 50 MB L2 at about 12 TB/s, but when I re-measured I found my NCU runs had flushed L2 before every launch and my timing had a launch floor near 15.5 µs. L2 residency is one of two causes, the cache holds about 36 MB in practice, and it serves these weights at 3.5 to 4.6 TB/s; the sections below use the corrected numbers.
  </Note>

  Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) is the attention design in DeepSeek-V2 and V3. Each token's KV cache entry is a 512-dimensional latent plus a 64-dimensional RoPE key, 576 values in all, instead of full per-head keys and values, so the attention kernel reads far less memory.

  The latent still has to meet the per-head query and value spaces somewhere. In the weight-absorbed form that serving engines use, that happens in two batched matmuls per layer, and they run every layer for every token with weight matrices that do not depend on batch size or sequence length. Attention kernel benchmarks time the kernel on its own, so these BMMs fall outside them. I wanted to know how much time they take, and whether quantizing their weights would make them cheaper.
</Section>

<Section id="absorbed-bmms" title="The absorbed projection BMMs">
  With weight absorption, MLA never builds full K or V. BMM1 multiplies each head's query by that head's key up-projection, so attention scores are computed directly against the 512-dimensional latent. BMM2 takes the attention output, which is still in latent space, and multiplies it by the value up-projection to get back to each head's 128-dimensional output. On DeepSeek-V3 shapes (128 heads, 61 layers) each one is a \[128, bs, 128] × \[128, 128, 512] batched GEMM or its transpose: 128 independent small matmuls.

  The weight per BMM is 128 × 128 × 512 × 2 bytes = 16 MB in FP16, and it is the same at every batch size.

  I timed both BMMs separately from the FlashInfer MLA attention kernel with per-launch CUDA events, in eager mode. At batch size 1 the two BMMs take 35.6 µs per layer against 23.0 µs for the attention kernel, so they are 61% of attention-layer time, or 2.17 ms per token across 61 layers. That share drops as batch size grows: the BMM time stays near 35 µs through bs=128 while attention time grows with the batch.

  These are eager-mode numbers, and they carry the launch floor described below. With CUDA-graph timing a single 16 MB BMM takes about 4.8 µs instead of about 17 µs, so an engine that captures decode in a graph will see a much smaller absolute cost. The ratio to attention moves less, since the attention kernel carries the same floor.

  By arithmetic intensity, every one of these BMMs is memory-bound: intensity reaches 93 only at bs=1024, against a crossover of 295 on H100 (989 TFLOPS over 3.35 TB/s).

</Section>

<Section id="int4" title="Trying weight-only INT4">
  The weights dominate the bytes moved, so the obvious move is to shrink them. Under an HBM-only roofline, reading 4 MB instead of 16 MB predicts a 3.9× speedup at bs=1.

  First I checked quality. On DeepSeek-V2-Lite (15.7B, same MLA architecture), wikitext-2 perplexity is 5.727 in FP16, 5.777 with only the absorbed projection weights in INT4 (+0.051), and 11.784 with every linear layer in INT4 (+6.057). Quantizing just these weights costs very little.

  <Figure
    src="/mla-profiling/ppl.webp"
    alt="Perplexity for FP16, selective INT4, and all-linear INT4"
    caption="Wikitext-2 perplexity on DeepSeek-V2-Lite. INT4 on the absorbed projection weights adds 0.051. INT4 on every linear layer more than doubles perplexity."
  />

  Then I wrote a batched W4A16 Triton kernel: the head dimension goes into the launch grid so all 128 heads run in one launch, INT4 weights are unpacked to FP16 in registers, and the matmul uses `tl.dot` on tensor cores. Throughout this page, speedup means FP16 time divided by INT4 time, so anything under 1× means INT4 is slower.

  With event timing at bs=1, cuBLAS FP16 took 0.036 ms and my INT4 kernel 0.073 ms: a speedup of 0.49×, not 3.9×. The speedup fell further at larger batch sizes.

</Section>

<Section id="measurement" title="What I got wrong in the first measurement">
  My first explanation was that the 16 MB weights fit in the 50 MB L2, get served at about 12 TB/s, and so INT4 saves HBM bandwidth that was never the bottleneck. I supported that with a weight-size sweep and an NCU sweep. When I went back to check it, three parts of the evidence did not hold.

  The NCU counters were all cold-cache. Kernel replay defaults to `--cache-control all`, which flushes the caches before every measured launch, so none of those runs could see L2 residency at any size. Re-collected with `--cache-control none` in a warm loop, `torch.bmm` re-reads 4.0 MB from DRAM per launch at the 16 MB shape, so about 75% of the weight bytes come from L2. At 32 MB it re-reads 2.2 MB. At 40 MB and above, essentially the whole weight comes from DRAM again.

  So the effective capacity is about 36 MB, not the nominal 50 MB, and the cliff sits between 32 and 40 MB. The effective serving bandwidth below the cliff is 3.5 to 4.6 TB/s, with an incremental slope of 6.3 TB/s. That is above the 3.35 TB/s HBM peak, which is only possible if L2 serves part of it, but it is nowhere near 12 TB/s.

  The timing had a floor. Per-launch CUDA-event timing on this stack bottoms out near 15.5 µs: a 0.5 MB bmm times the same as a 16 MB one. That floor made FP16 latency look flat from 8 to 32 MB, which I had read as L2 serving. Timed inside a CUDA graph, the same FP16 kernel goes from 3.5 µs at 8 MB to 7.3 µs at 32 MB, then jumps to 16.6 µs at 40 MB.

  What I check now, before believing a number like these:

  - the `--cache-control` setting, before reading any residency claim off NCU;
  - whether two supposedly different cache conditions give the same counters, which means the setup is wrong before it means the effect is absent;
  - a trivially small version of the same op in the same harness, which tells me whether I am timing the floor;
  - CUDA-graph timing for microsecond kernels, especially when comparing Triton to cuBLAS;
  - time converted to bytes per second and compared to HBM peak, since above peak means a cache is helping and also bounds how much;
  - a fixed shape with only the cache changed, by rotating weight copies, rather than a size sweep that changes the kernel too;
  - the software versions, written next to every number.
</Section>

<Section id="two-causes" title="Two causes, not one">
  The test that separates the causes holds the 16 MB MLA shape fixed and cycles each launch through k independent weight copies inside a CUDA graph. With one or two copies the working set fits in the effective cache. With six copies it is 96 MB and FP16 has to go to HBM every time.

  | Configuration (16 MB shape, bs=1, graph-timed) | Time per BMM | Speedup vs FP16 L2-warm |
  | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ----------------------- |
  | FP16, weights in L2 (1 copy)                   | 4.8 µs       | 1.00×                   |
  | FP16, forced to HBM (6 copies)                 | 8.3 µs       | 0.58×                   |
  | INT4 W4A16 (1 or 6 copies)                     | 9.1 to 9.3 µs | 0.52×                  |

  INT4 barely notices the cache state. FP16 slows by 3.5 µs when forced to HBM, which matches reloading the roughly 12 MB (75%) that L2 had been serving. Even then INT4 is still slower: 0.89× of FP16 with FP16 reading from HBM. L2 residency explains why the speedup falls from 0.89× to 0.52×, but removing it would not make this kernel win.

  The rest is the kernel itself. Reading 4.2 MB at HBM rate plus fixed cost should take about 3.4 µs, and it takes 9.1 to 9.3 µs. It is bound by dequantization instructions, not by memory or by useful math:

  - every packed byte goes through a scalar mask, shift, select and convert before it can feed a tensor core;
  - `BLOCK_M=16` tiles at M=1 pad away 15/16 of the tensor-core work;
  - the even/odd nibble interleave costs two `tl.dot` calls per K block.

  Cold-cache NCU is still fine for classifying the kernels, and it agrees. Across the size sweep the FP16 cuBLAS kernel stays below 15% SM utilization while DRAM utilization rises from 35% to 83%, and the INT4 kernel runs 33% to 79% SM utilization with DRAM under 23%. Useful compute never exceeds about 4.6 TFLOPS, 0.5% of peak, so the high SM utilization in the INT4 kernel is unpacking work, not FLOPs.

  The size sweep, re-timed with CUDA graphs, tells the same story from the other side. INT4's speedup is about 0.5× at 16 MB and rises to about 0.9 to 1.0× once the weights pass the cliff at 40 MB and above. It reaches parity and never wins. The event-timed version of this sweep does not travel across software versions: at 16 MB the INT4/FP16 time ratio was 1.86× on Triton 3.5.1 and 2.75× on Triton 3.3.0, and at 128 MB 1.08× against 1.58×. The direction held and the numbers did not, because event timing at bs=1 mostly measures launch overhead, which is what changes with the stack. I only trust the graph-timed numbers.
</Section>

<Section id="w8a8" title="What does win">
  If dequantization is the in-core limit, a kernel without it should win exactly where the weights stream from HBM and lose where they sit in L2. I tested that with a W8A8 Triton kernel: per-channel INT8 weights, per-token INT8 activations from one small extra kernel, an inner loop that is only `tl.dot(int8, int8)` into INT32, and scales applied once on the final accumulator. Relative error is about 0.009, and the timings include the activation-quant kernel.

  | Weights     | FP16 (µs) | W8A8 (µs) | W4A16 (µs) | W8A8 speedup |
  | ----------- | --------- | --------- | ---------- | ------------ |
  | 8 MB        | 3.6       | 5.1       | 5.5        | 0.70×        |
  | 16 MB (MLA) | 4.9       | 6.9       | 9.0        | 0.70×        |
  | 32 MB       | 7.6       | 10.4      | 15.6       | 0.73×        |
  | 48 MB       | 21.7      | 14.3      | 22.5       | 1.52×        |
  | 64 MB       | 27.5      | 17.9      | 29.0       | 1.53×        |
  | 96 MB       | 38.6      | 28.2      | 41.8       | 1.37×        |
  | 128 MB      | 49.1      | 34.4      | 55.7       | 1.43×        |

  Graph-timed, bs=1. The flip happens between 32 and 48 MB, at the measured effective capacity rather than the nominal 50 MB, and W8A8 beats W4A16 at every size. At the 16 MB MLA shape FP16 still wins in this loop. Stacking several 16 MB layers into one decode step crosses the same line: FP16 wins through two layers (32 MB) and W8A8 wins from three (48 MB). At large batch sizes the workload turns compute-bound and cuBLAS wins again.
</Section>

<Section id="methodology" title="Checking the setup first">
  Before the MLA work I compared FlashInfer and Triton attention on Llama-3-8B GQA, where the gap is already known, to make sure my harness reproduced it. In decode FlashInfer reached 89% of H100's HBM bandwidth and Triton 80%. In prefill FlashInfer reached 552 TFLOPS against Triton's 209. The NCU explanation is mostly about load paths: FlashInfer's Hopper kernel moves Q, K and V with TMA into shared memory, while the Triton kernel I compared issues ordinary `ld.global` loads. That is a property of that kernel, not a limit of Triton. It also makes naive L1 hit rates meaningless here, since TMA traffic bypasses L1. FlashInfer also gets more DRAM throughput from fewer active warps (183 registers per thread, 12.2% active warps against Triton's 35.4%), so occupancy was not what separated them.
</Section>

<Section id="takeaways" title="Takeaways">
  At small batch sizes in eager mode, MLA's absorbed projection BMMs cost more than the attention kernel they feed. They are tiny, fixed-weight, launch-floor-dominated operations, and a single-level roofline badly mispredicts them.

  Weight-only INT4 with a dequantization-heavy kernel cannot win at the MLA shape. Dequantization overhead keeps the kernel at about 2.7× its memory cost, and in a warm single-operator loop L2 residency takes away what is left of the upside. Quantization starts to pay only when the working set is larger than the roughly 36 MB that L2 actually holds and the kernel skips per-element dequantization.

  The bigger lesson for me was about measurement. For microsecond kernels, I now time inside CUDA graphs, collect cache-residency counters with `--cache-control none`, and test a causal story by removing the cause at a fixed shape before I believe it.

  All of the L2 results on this page come from a warm loop that runs one operator on the same weights again and again. A full decode step reads each layer's 16 MB weights once, with the rest of the model's weights passing through L2 in between, so in serving these weights stream from HBM. There the comparison that applies is the forced-HBM row above, where INT4 is 0.89× of FP16, and the stacking result, where W8A8 wins.
</Section>
