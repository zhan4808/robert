The first three parts of this series leaned on hundreds of vLLM runs across 4, 8 and 16 GPUs. Many of those runs were wrong the first time. This is the field guide I wish I'd had at the start: how to read what a run is doing, and how to tell a real number from an artifact.

<Note title="summary">

- All-reduce size depends on what's in the step: kilobytes for decode at low load, tens of megabytes for prefill.
- At high load, the batch is usually capped by KV-cache capacity, and you can predict the cap from a number vLLM prints at startup.
- Most wrong numbers come from a short list of traps. There's a checklist at the end.

</Note>

## What one step sends

A serving step is prefill, decode, or a mix of both. The number of all-reduces per step is fixed by the model (around a hundred for the MoE models we ran). Their size depends on what's in the step:

- **Decode:** tokens in the step = running requests. Each message is running requests × hidden size × 2 bytes in bf16. Small at low load, megabytes at high load.
- **Prefill:** tokens in the step = the chunk budget (`max_num_batched_tokens`). At 8,192 tokens and hidden size 4096, that's 64 MiB per all-reduce. Prefill messages are the large ones.
- **Mixed steps** carry a prefill chunk plus every running decode token, so their messages sit in between.

That's why a single microbenchmark point can't model a serving run. One session sweeps from kilobytes to tens of megabytes, and the best NCCL setting changes along the way ([part 2](/blog/nccl-defaults-off-nvlink)).

## What caps the batch: usually the KV cache, and you can predict it

At high concurrency, the number of requests actually running is often far below the number you sent. The usual cause is KV-cache capacity, not compute and not the scheduler. Every running request holds cache for all of its tokens, input and output, so the arithmetic is simple:

$$
\text{max running requests} \approx \frac{\text{KV pool tokens}}{\text{tokens per request (input + output)}}
$$

vLLM prints the KV pool size at startup. In our runs, this estimate matched the served batch within a few percent across three layouts. Two consequences follow:

- **Past the cap, more concurrency means more queueing, not more throughput.** The throughput curve flattens and latency climbs. If you're benchmarking beyond the cap, you're measuring the queue.
- **A layout that grows the pool can win at high load even if each step is slower.** Two independent replicas each get their own pool. For MLA models, pipeline parallelism grows the pool and tensor parallelism doesn't ([part 3](/blog/pcie-vs-ethernet-scale-up)).

## Ten ways a benchmark point lies

Each of these cost us real GPU time. They fall into four groups, roughly in the order you'll meet them.

### The profiler

- **Eager mode invents communication.** Profiling with `--enforce-eager` counts launch gaps as collective time and inflates the step many times over. Use eager runs for message sizes and counts only, and time everything in CUDA-graph mode.
- **Graph-replayed collectives are invisible to the NCCL profiler.** A replayed graph emits no events. That's why sizes have to come from eager runs.

### The server

- **vLLM v1 doesn't log preemptions.** A deck of ours said “zero preemptions” until `/metrics` showed thousands. Read `vllm:num_preemptions_total`, and for anything cache-related, watch the prefix-cache hit rate.
- **Prefix caching plus a fixed seed inflates low-concurrency points.** Repeated prompts hit the cache. Pass `--no-enable-prefix-caching` for synthetic sweeps.
- **Autotune caches can deadlock multi-rank startup.** If one rank finds a cached kernel tuning while the others start tuning and wait on each other, startup hangs. Give each multi-host run a fresh autotune cache, or turn autotuning off.

### The client and your scripts

- **Open-file limits fail requests silently.** An ssh session often inherits a limit of 1,024 open files. At high concurrency the benchmark client fails most of its requests and still prints a plausible throughput. Raise the limit (`ulimit -n 65536`), and always check failed and completed counts against the number of prompts.
- **A run that finishes much faster than its twin probably crashed.** Check the logs before you celebrate.
- **`pgrep -f` matches itself.** Inside `ssh host '...'` or `bash -c '...'`, the wrapper's own command line contains the pattern, so wait loops never end and `pkill` kills its own shell. Bracket one character: `'vllm serv[e]'`.

### The machine

- **Clocks and power caps move results.** A power cap throttled prefill far more than decode. Record clock and power state with every result.
- **Shared machines contaminate results.** Someone else's job on the same GPUs or NICs will quietly change your numbers. Check for other processes before every run, not just the first.

## Profiling overhead is real but small

Nsight Systems added a fraction of a millisecond per step. That's noticeable at one request, where a step is short, and negligible at high load. Compare profiled steps with profiled steps, and quote client-side throughput from unprofiled runs.

## A checklist before you trust a point

- Graph mode, with the profiler off for the throughput number
- Failed = 0, and completed = prompts sent
- Running requests compared with the KV-cap estimate: are you measuring the queue?
- Prefix caching off for synthetic prompts
- Clock and power state recorded
- NCCL's algorithm and protocol picks logged (`NCCL_DEBUG_SUBSYS=TUNING`)
- Cross-host: more than one launch per setting
- Nobody else on the machines

That's the end of the series. The short version of all four parts: without NVLink, communication cost is mostly fixed per-call latency, NCCL's defaults don't know your fabric, the fabric should decide your layout, and none of it means anything until you've checked the measurement.
