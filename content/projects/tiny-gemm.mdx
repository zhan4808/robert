<Section id="motivation" title="Motivation">
  Decoding runs one token at a time. With a batch of 1 to 8, every linear layer is a skinny GEMM: a few rows of activations against a full weight matrix. Each weight is read once and used for a handful of multiply-adds, so the time goes to streaming weights out of DRAM and to the fixed cost of each launch, not to arithmetic.

  That makes weight-only INT4 look like a free win. An FP16 weight is 2 bytes and a packed INT4 weight is half a byte, so the kernel reads 4x fewer weight bytes, and a purely bandwidth-bound kernel would run up to 4x faster. In practice some layers got close to that and some got slower than FP16. I built Tiny-GEMM to find out which, and why.
</Section>

<Section id="kernel" title="The Kernel">
  The kernel is written in Triton. Weights are quantized per tensor and packed two INT4 values to a byte. Each tile of packed weights is loaded from DRAM, unpacked to floating point in registers, and multiplied against the activations with FP32 accumulation. Tile configurations are static, keyed by shape family and batch bucket; decode shapes cluster tightly enough that a small lookup table was enough and avoided autotuning at runtime.

  The dot products run in FP32 rather than on tensor cores. INT4 tensor-core MMA would not help a weight-only kernel anyway: Ampere's INT4 MMA multiplies INT4 by INT4, so it needs quantized activations too, and Hopper has no INT4 MMA at all. The realistic fast path for weight-only INT4 is to unpack to FP16 in registers and use FP16 tensor-core MMA, which this kernel does not do yet.

  I compared three versions of every shape: FP16 through torch.matmul (cuBLAS), a dequantized baseline that unpacks INT4 to an FP16 tensor and then runs an FP16 GEMM, and the fused INT4 kernel. The dequantized baseline separates the cost of the format from the cost of doing the unpack inside the GEMM.
</Section>

<Section id="setup" title="Setup">
  Everything ran on one NVIDIA A10G. Shapes use Llama-3-8B-style dimensions with hidden size 4096. K is the input width and N the output width. Q and O projections are 4096 by 4096. K and V projections have N = 1024 because grouped-query attention gives them 8 heads of 128 instead of 32. FFN up (and gate) go from 4096 to 14336, and FFN down goes back.

  | Layer                | M      | K     | N     | FP16 weights | INT4 weights |
  | -------------------- | ------ | ----- | ----- | ------------ | ------------ |
  | Q / O proj           | 1 to 8 | 4096  | 4096  | 33.6 MB      | 8.4 MB       |
  | K / V proj (GQA)     | 1 to 8 | 4096  | 1024  | 8.4 MB       | 2.1 MB       |
  | FFN up / gate        | 1 to 8 | 4096  | 14336 | 117.4 MB     | 29.4 MB      |
  | FFN down             | 1 to 8 | 14336 | 4096  | 117.4 MB     | 29.4 MB      |

  M from 1 to 8 is the decode range; the width sweep below also runs out to M = 64. Each latency is the median of 50 runs after 10 warmup iterations, timed in the benchmark harness, since profilers replay kernels and inflate timings. Nsight Compute supplied hardware counters and Nsight Systems the per-kernel time breakdown.
</Section>

<Section id="what-decides" title="What Decides the Win">
  Both kernels read the same weight matrix, one at 2 bytes per value and one at half a byte. If weight reads dominate, the speedup is 4 times the ratio of how fast each kernel moves its weight bytes:

  $$
  \text{speedup} \approx 4 \times \frac{\text{INT4 weight bytes per second}}{\text{FP16 weight bytes per second}}
  $$

  Read the table that way. FFN up at 3.58x means INT4 moved its bytes at about 90% of the rate cuBLAS moved FP16's. K/V proj at 0.62x means INT4 moved its bytes at about a sixth of FP16's rate. The question is what slows INT4 down on small shapes.

  The first answer is a per-launch floor. INT4 latency barely moves from K/V proj to Q/O proj, 0.043 to 0.047 ms, while its weight bytes quadruple. Below roughly 10 MB of traffic the INT4 points in the scatter below sit on a flat floor, and FP16 on the K/V shape finishes under it. A launch has to move enough weight bytes for the 4x saving to outgrow that floor. The dequantization microbenchmark in the paper shows the unpack path is a large share of K/V's runtime.

  <Figure
    src="/tiny-gemm/memory_traffic_scatter.webp"
    alt="Latency versus estimated bytes moved for FP16 and Tiny-GEMM INT4 across decode shapes"
    caption="Latency against estimated bytes moved. Each INT4 point sits at a quarter of its FP16 shape's traffic. Small INT4 launches sit on a floor near 0.05 ms however few bytes they move."
  />

  Bytes alone don't explain FFN down. It has exactly the same weight bytes as FFN up and got 1.69x instead of 3.58x. The difference is where the parallelism comes from. With M between 1 and 8 there is a single row of output tiles, so without split-K the number of CTAs is set by N. FFN up has 14336 output columns to spread across the A10G's 80 SMs. FFN down has 4096, so it launches about 3.5 times fewer CTAs and each one walks a K that is 3.5 times longer. My reading is that too few CTAs per SM leaves too few loads in flight to keep DRAM busy, and that is what split-K would fix.

  The paper framed this with an arithmetic-intensity threshold of about 8 FLOPs/byte. I dropped it. A decode GEMM does 2MKN FLOPs over about 2KN bytes of FP16 weights, so its intensity is about M FLOPs/byte for FP16 and 4M for INT4, whatever N is. At M = 1, K/V proj and FFN up have the same intensity and opposite results, so intensity can't be what decides. Weight bytes per launch and CTAs per SM, measured against the fixed per-launch cost, are what the data actually tracks.

  Prefill fits the same picture. With M in the hundreds, each launch has many rows of output tiles and far more work to spread the fixed cost over. The paper measures FFN up at about 3.0x in prefill and K/V proj rising to about 2.3x, from 0.62x in decode with the same kernel and the same weights. A quantization policy tuned on prefill throughput can therefore slow down decode on the same model.
</Section>

<Section id="hardware" title="What the Counters Show">
  Nsight Compute shows FP16 decode GEMMs running at about 75 to 77% of peak DRAM bandwidth with low compute utilization, as expected for a kernel that does almost no arithmetic per byte.

  <Figure

    alt="Nsight Compute comparison of FP16 and INT4: DRAM bandwidth, SM throughput, warps active and tensor instruction share"
    caption="Nsight Compute counters for FP16 and INT4. FP16 is pinned on DRAM bandwidth. INT4 is low on both DRAM and SM throughput, and its tensor-instruction share is near zero because its dot products run in FP32."
  />

  The INT4 kernel is not bound by memory or by arithmetic. It moves a quarter of the bytes but uses only a fraction of the bandwidth, and its SM throughput is lower than FP16's, not higher. Very few warps are active in either kernel. cuBLAS keeps DRAM busy anyway; my kernel doesn't, which points at latency and occupancy rather than the unpack arithmetic being expensive in itself. That is the same story as the FFN down result.
</Section>

<Section id="where-time-goes" title="Where the Time Goes">
  The shapes INT4 helps most are the ones that matter most. In a Llama-3-8B-style layer, the three FFN matrices hold 3 × 4096 × 14336 weights against 2 × 4096 × 4096 + 2 × 4096 × 1024 for attention, so FFN is about 80% of the weight bytes every decode step has to stream.

<Section id="limitations" title="Limitations">
  The quantization is per tensor: one scale for the whole weight matrix. Deployed INT4 uses group-wise scales, typically one per 128 weights, because per-tensor scales lose too much accuracy, and group-wise scales add scale loads and multiplies to the inner loop. My numbers are optimistic for that reason, and I didn't measure model accuracy at all. I also didn't compare against the INT4 kernels people actually deploy, such as Marlin, AWQ's kernels, or tinygemm, so this shows when INT4 beats cuBLAS FP16 with this kernel, not where it stands among INT4 kernels. Everything ran on one GPU.
</Section>

<Section id="takeaways" title="Takeaways">
  Don't apply INT4 uniformly. On this kernel at K = 4096, INT4 lost below about N = 2048 and won above it, and the result held from M = 1 to 8, so the choice can be made per layer when the model loads. FFN up and gate are worth quantizing. K/V proj under GQA is narrow enough that FP16 is faster. FFN down shows that enough bytes are not sufficient: the launch also needs enough CTAs to keep memory busy.

  The broader lesson for me was that cutting bytes only buys latency if the kernel can still keep DRAM saturated. A 4x reduction in weight traffic turned into anything from 0.62x to 3.58x depending on whether the launch was big and parallel enough to hide its fixed costs.

  ### What's next

  - Unpack to FP16 in registers and use FP16 tensor-core MMA, the path that works on both Ampere and Hopper.
  - Split-K for M = 1, so narrow-N and long-K shapes like FFN down launch enough CTAs.
  - Group-wise scales, and a head-to-head against Marlin on the same shapes.
  - FP4 and FP8 on Blackwell, where tcgen05.mma.kind::f8f6f4 does low-precision MMA in hardware and changes where the line falls.
</Section>
