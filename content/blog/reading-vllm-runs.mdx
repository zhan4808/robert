The first three parts of this series leaned on hundreds of vLLM runs across 4, 8 and 16 GPUs, and a lot of those runs were wrong the first time. This is the field guide I wish I'd had at the start: how to read what a run is actually doing, and how to tell a real number from an artifact.

## What one step sends

A serving step is prefill, decode, or a mix of the two. The number of all-reduces per step is fixed by the model (around a hundred for the MoE models we ran), but their size depends on what's in the step.

In a decode step, the tokens in the step are just the running requests, so each message is running requests × hidden size × 2 bytes in bf16: tiny at low load, a few megabytes at high load. In a prefill step, the tokens are the chunk budget (`max_num_batched_tokens`); at 8,192 tokens and hidden size 4096 that's 64 MiB per all-reduce. Mixed steps carry a prefill chunk plus every running decode token, so they land in between.

That's why no single microbenchmark point can stand in for a serving run. One session sweeps from kilobytes to tens of megabytes, and the best NCCL setting changes along the way, as [part 2](/blog/nccl-defaults-off-nvlink) showed.

## What caps the batch

At high concurrency, the number of requests actually running is often far below the number you sent. The usual cause is KV-cache capacity, not compute and not the scheduler. Every running request holds cache for all of its tokens, input and output, so the arithmetic is simple:

$$
\text{max running requests} \approx \frac{\text{KV pool tokens}}{\text{tokens per request (input + output)}}
$$

vLLM prints the KV pool size at startup, and in our runs this estimate matched the served batch to within a few percent across three layouts.

<Diagram name="kv-cap" caption="What the KV-cache cap does to a sweep. Past it, extra concurrency waits in a queue: throughput stays flat and latency climbs. Illustrative." />

Two things follow. Past the cap, more concurrency means more queueing, not more throughput, so if you're benchmarking beyond it you're measuring the queue. And a layout that grows the pool can win at high load even if each step is slower: two independent replicas each get their own pool, and for MLA models pipeline parallelism grows the pool while tensor parallelism doesn't ([part 3](/blog/pcie-vs-ethernet-scale-up)).

## Ten ways a number lies

Each of these cost us real GPU time. Roughly in the order you'll run into them:

### The profiler

Eager mode invents communication. Profiling with `--enforce-eager` counts the gaps between kernel launches as collective time and inflates the step many times over. Use eager runs for message sizes and counts only, and time everything in CUDA-graph mode.

<Diagram name="eager-vs-graph" caption="Why eager-mode profiles overstate communication: launch gaps turn into spin time inside the collective, and graph replay removes them." />

The catch is that graph-replayed collectives are invisible to the NCCL profiler, because a replayed graph emits no events. That's the reason sizes have to come from eager runs in the first place.

### The server

vLLM v1 doesn't log preemptions. A slide deck of ours said "zero preemptions" right up until `/metrics` showed thousands; read `vllm:num_preemptions_total`, and for anything cache-related, watch the prefix-cache hit rate too.

Prefix caching plus a fixed random seed inflates low-concurrency points, because repeated prompts hit the cache. Pass `--no-enable-prefix-caching` for synthetic sweeps.

Autotune caches can deadlock multi-rank startup. If one rank finds a cached kernel tuning while the others start tuning and wait on each other, startup just hangs. Give each multi-host run a fresh autotune cache, or turn autotuning off.

### The client and your scripts

Open-file limits fail requests silently. An ssh session often inherits a limit of 1,024 open files, and at high concurrency the benchmark client fails most of its requests and still prints a plausible throughput. Raise the limit (`ulimit -n 65536`) and always check failed and completed counts against the number of prompts.

A run that finishes much faster than its twin probably crashed. Check the logs before celebrating.

And `pgrep -f` matches itself. Inside `ssh host '...'` or `bash -c '...'`, the wrapper's own command line contains the pattern, so wait loops never end and `pkill` kills its own shell. Bracket one character: `'vllm serv[e]'`.

### The machine

Clocks and power caps move results; one power cap throttled prefill far more than decode, so record clock and power state with every result. And shared machines contaminate results: someone else's job on the same GPUs or NICs will quietly change your numbers, so check for other processes before every run, not just the first.

## Profiling overhead is real, but small

Nsight Systems added a fraction of a millisecond per step. That's noticeable at one request, where a step is short, and negligible at high load. Compare profiled steps with profiled steps, and quote client-side throughput from unprofiled runs.

## Before you trust a number

- Graph mode, with the profiler off for the throughput number
- Zero failed requests, and completed equals prompts sent
- Running requests checked against the KV-cap estimate
- Prefix caching off for synthetic prompts
- Clock and power state recorded
- NCCL's algorithm and protocol picks logged (`NCCL_DEBUG_SUBSYS=TUNING`)
- More than one launch per setting, if any rank crosses hosts
- Nobody else on the machines

That's the series. If I had to compress all four parts into a sentence: without NVLink, communication cost is mostly fixed per-call latency, NCCL's defaults don't know your fabric, the fabric should decide your layout, and none of it means anything until you've checked the measurement.
